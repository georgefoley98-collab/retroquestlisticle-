7 Reasons People Are Quietly Rediscovering the Games They Grew Up With

MB
Marcus Bell · Games & Culture Editor
Updated recently · 6 min read

There's a strange thing happening in living rooms and on commutes lately. Grown adults — the kind with jobs, kids, mortgages and absolutely no time — are going back to the games they played when they were ten. Not out of irony. Not for a laugh. Because something about those games still works in a way that modern ones, for all their power, somehow don't.

I spent a few weeks looking into why. I read the threads, talked to the people doing it, and tried it myself. What I found wasn't really about games at all. Here are the seven reasons that kept coming up.

01 The games they loved never actually got better

Ask anyone who grew up on the classics and they'll tell you the same thing: the games were finished. You bought one, and it was the whole thing — no updates downloading, no season pass, no "part two sold separately." A platformer from thirty years ago is exactly as good today as it was then. That's rarer than it sounds. Most things from our childhood don't hold up. These do.

"I went back expecting nostalgia to be doing the heavy lifting. It wasn't. The games are just genuinely brilliant."

02 Phones ruined retro games — then quietly un-ruined them

For years the only way back to these games was an app on your phone. And everyone who tried it says the same thing: it's miserable. Sliding your thumb across glass to play a game built for a D-pad is like eating soup with a fork. The magic of these games lives in the buttons — the click, the feedback, the muscle memory. Take that away and you've got a museum piece, not a game.

What changed is that small dedicated handhelds brought the real thing back: proper buttons, dual sticks, a screen built for the format. Suddenly the games feel the way they're supposed to feel again.

03 "Setup" used to be the dealbreaker — now there isn't any

Here's the quiet barrier that stopped most people for years: the fear of faff. The forums full of firmware, file formats and acronyms nobody wants to learn. For anyone who just wanted to play, it felt like homework.

The thing that flipped it for most people was the arrival of handhelds that come with everything already on them — tested, loaded, charged. You turn it on and pick a game. That's the entire process. "If you can use a TV remote, you can use this" is the line I heard more than once, and it's true.

04 It's the rare screen you don't have to feel guilty about

This one surprised me most, because it came up again and again from parents. A device with no internet connection is, these days, almost a novelty. No ads. No chat with strangers. No in-app purchases quietly draining a card. No algorithm deciding what your kid sees next. Just the same games the parents themselves grew up on.

"It's the only screen in the house I don't have to police. He plays the exact game I played at his age, and I'm not watching over his shoulder."

For a lot of families it's become the thing they reach for on long journeys — the one that keeps the back seat quiet without keeping anyone connected to anything.

05 It gives people something to share again

Almost everyone I spoke to had a second person in the story. A brother they used to fight for Player 1. A partner who grew up on the same games. A kid who suddenly wanted to know what Mum and Dad played "in the old days." These games were built for the sofa, for two people and one screen, and that hasn't changed.

More than a few people didn't buy one. They bought two.

06 It's the opposite of the doomscroll

There's a specific kind of tiredness that comes from an evening lost to a feed — stimulated but somehow emptier than before. Several people described swapping that out for twenty minutes of an old game instead. Same screen time. Completely different feeling. One person put it perfectly: the phone wants your attention; the game just wants you to have fun. No notifications. No feed. Nowhere else to be.

"I replaced my bedtime scroll with one level a night. It's the most relaxed I've felt in years, and I sleep better for it."

07 You own it — and that's the whole point

Modern gaming increasingly asks you to rent your own memories back: a subscription here, a library that comes and goes, servers that switch off. There's something quietly satisfying about a device where the games are simply on it. No account. No renewal. No expiry. You turn it on in five years and it's exactly the same. In a world of things that expire, people are drawn to something that doesn't.
